If you’re over 45, creatine has probably shown up in your feed this year. It used to live in black tubs at the back of the gym. Now it’s in gummies, stick packs and pastel jars, and everyone from your trainer to your doctor is talking about it.

Here’s the short version.

Creatine is a compound your muscles use for quick bursts of energy. Your body makes some and you get some from food, but supplementing tops up your muscle stores. Paired with strength training, it’s one of the most researched supplements there is, with decades of studies behind it. That matters more as we age, because muscle and strength slowly decline over time. What surprised me is that muscles aren’t the only place creatine works. Your brain uses it for energy too, and newer research has looked at creatine for memory, focus and mental fatigue, particularly in older adults and on short-sleep days. That research is a big part of why so many women in midlife are adding it to their routine. It’s one of the few supplements that might help both your body and your brain.

What creatine can’t do is work if you stop taking it. It only works when your muscles stay topped up, which means taking it every day. In my experience, most people don’t just quit when a product failed. They also quit because they got tired of chalky powder or forgot the gummies by week two. I tried or dug into 6 of the most talked-about options. Here’s where each one landed:

Here’s my history with creatine powders: I’d buy a tub, use it for about ten days, and then leave it in the pantry. It wasn’t that it didn’t work. I know it works, it’s one of the most studied supplements out there. It was that a spoonful of gritty white powder in my morning coffee started to feel like a chore. So when I tried the Wildtype product, my first question was about whether I’d actually want to drink it.

I did.

It’s a berry hibiscus tea, a balance of tart and sweet and I like it best over ice. Two scoops go into a glass of water, and because there’s a small electrolyte blend in there, it reads more like a post-workout drink than a supplement. It’s the first creatine I’ve reached for because I wanted it, not because I was supposed to.

Did some digging: The formula is built around 3g of creatine monohydrate, the daily amount research shows will fully top up your muscles in about four weeks without a loading phase, plus 400mg of creatine HCl. That’s less than the 5g most tubs use, which I’d weigh if you’re a heavy lifter. But for everyday strength, it’s the amount studied for keeping muscles topped up.

The creatine is micronized, so it mixes faster and feels smoother than standard powder, and it’s rounded out with L-glutamine and a light electrolyte blend to provide recovery benefits beyond the creatine.

The reason this wins for me is the routine. For the first time, I’ve actually taken creatine every single day for more than a few days. For something that only works with daily use, that was the deciding factor.

Did some digging: Creatine gummies had a rough couple of years. Create did better than most. It passed a 2025 test, though a 2024 test flagged it below its label. That’s the catch with gummies: you’re trusting that what’s on the label survived the shelf.

At the price, powder still wins for me on reliability.
